
# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper investigates the extent to which visual similarity in modern computer vision representations explains human word extension and artificial word learning, and what the composition of teaching examples contributes beyond similarity. Combining a trial-level reanalysis of a large adult word-extension dataset (Lewis & Frank, 2018; 520 participants, 172,800 decisions) with controlled few-shot naming experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and comparisons against three human similarity benchmarks (THINGS, ImageNet-HSJ, and CIFAR-10H) across ten representations (eight trained vision encoders, a randomly initialized ResNet-50, and raw pixels), the author shows that unweighted distance to the centroid of teaching examples predicts within-domain word extension and artificial naming accuracy across most encoders—including the untrained network—but does not capture the dramatic shift in human generalization driven by example composition.

## Strengths
* The manuscript provides a rigorous and valuable methodological baseline by evaluating ten diverse visual representations—spanning self-supervised transformers, vision-language models, child-egocentric video models, supervised architectures, and untrained/pixel controls—directly on the exact trial-level stimulus images and behavioral decisions of the suspicious-coincidence word-extension paradigm.
* Including a randomly initialized ResNet-50 alongside raw pixels serves as an insightful diagnostic control, demonstrating that hierarchical convolutional architectures possess spatial inductive biases that yield both a significant negative distance slope in adult word extension and positive correlations with human similarity datasets prior to any feature training.
* The paper demonstrates commendable transparency regarding research practices, clearly distinguishing between internally frozen plans, descriptive baselines, and post-hoc exploratory analyses, while providing comprehensive deviation logs and open code/data artifacts.

## Weaknesses
* The overarching theoretical conclusions conflate the limitations of a first-order summary statistic—unweighted cosine distance to the normalized centroid of the teaching examples—with the limits of "visual geometry" itself; in continuous metric spaces, narrow (three subordinate) versus broad (three superordinate) teaching sets differ markedly in their within-set dispersion (variance/span), which prior continuous Bayesian and deep neural network models of this paradigm explicitly use to modulate generalization breadth (Reference Paper: Generalization, Similarity, and Bayesian Inference, Joshua B. Tenenbaum and Thomas L. Griffiths, 2001; Reference Paper: Learning Hierarchical Visual Representations in Deep Neural Networks Using Hierarchical Linguistic Labels, Joshua C. Peterson, Paul Soulos, Aida Nematzadeh, and Thomas L. Griffiths, 2018).
* Several core components of the multi-encoder narrative and statistical decision framework—including the mathematical form of the "support-dependent acceptance rule" in Section 5, the two-stage permutation test for $\hat{\Delta}$ in Section 2.3, the "geometry calibration" and "geometry cutoffs" in Sections 2.4 and 4.2, and the Smallest Effect Size of Interest (SESOI) region rules (`S/I/N`) in Appendix Tables 11 and 12—are either omitted from the manuscript text or applied in ways where point-null significance ($p < .05$) is labeled as support across all effect-size margins (`.10/.20/.30`) even when point estimates and confidence intervals fall below those margins.
* In the adult generalized linear mixed-effects models (Section 2), omitting by-image random slopes for distance and omitting experiment-level random effects in the observation-level post-hoc trial-order model ($N = 172,800$) risks inflating Type I error rates for fixed-effect interactions, creating an unreconciled discrepancy between the highly significant observation-level order interaction ($p < .001$) and the inconclusive experiment-level primary permutation test ($N = 12$).
* The artificial learner distance gradient and dissociation results on CIFAR-10/100 (Section 3) are largely mechanical consequences of applying nearest-centroid classification to disjunctive class pairs in a purely visual space with no access to semantic graphs, while the empirical bridge testing whether human similarity distances predict learner difficulty (H2 in Section 4) is limited by small sample sizes ($n = 61$ and $n = 32$ non-independent pairs) and conflicting adjusted versus unadjusted classifications between the main text and Appendix Table 12 (Reference Paper: Prototypical Networks for Few-Shot Learning, Jake Snell, Kevin Swersky, and Richard Zemel, 2017; Reference Paper: Enriching ImageNet with Human Similarity Judgments and Psychological Embeddings, Brett D. Roads and Bradley C. Love, 2021; Reference Paper: Aligning Machine and Human Visual Representations Across Abstraction Levels, Lukas Muttenthaler, Klaus Greff, Fabian Born, Bernhard Spitzer, Simon Kornblith, Michael C. Mozer, Klaus-Robert Müller, Thomas Unterthiner, and Andrew K. Lampinen, 2025).
* The narrative concludes that human similarity alignment (H1) fails to separate trained encoders from the untrained ResNet-50 control based on binary significance gating ($p < .05$) and an undefined geometry calibration rule, which overlooks substantial quantitative separation in rank correlation magnitude on THINGS and ImageNet-HSJ ($\rho = .39\text{--}.70$ for non-autoencoder trained models vs. $\rho = .11$ for the untrained network) (Reference Paper: Getting Aligned on Representational Alignment, Ilia Sucholutsky, Lukas Muttenthaler, Adrian Weller, et al., 2025).

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Theoretical Motivation, and Multi-Encoder Narrative Synthesis] (Pages: 1-3, 16-20)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Clarity of the Multi-Encoder Narrative and Unmotivated Elevation of Presentation Order to the "Frozen Primary Question" (Abstract, p. 1; Section 1, pp. 1–3; Section 6, pp. 16–17; Section 7.1, pp. 17–18)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Because the manuscript layers multiple chronological stages of analysis—an initial DINOv2-small plan, a subsequent multi-encoder expansion, and post hoc exploratory additions—into a single narrative, readers encounter procedural shorthand and an unmotivated primary hypothesis early in the framing sections:
     - **Unmotivated elevation of presentation order to the "frozen primary question":** In the Abstract (p. 1) and Introduction (pp. 1–2), readers encounter *"Whether presentation order changes this slope, the frozen primary question, remained inconclusive ($\hat{\Delta} = 0.020$, 95% CI $[-0.205, 0.274]$)"* before "presentation order" or the Lewis and Frank (2018) between-experiment order manipulation has been introduced. Moreover, Section 1 never explains *why* trial order (whether the one-example trial preceded or followed the three-example trials across experiments) would be expected to change the slope of static visual distance across encoders, nor why this interaction was elevated to the primary confirmatory question of the multi-encoder study (relegating the main effect of visual distance to a "positive-control gate"; Footnote 1, p. 17) despite Section 6 (p. 16) noting that trial order is confounded with other between-experiment design differences.
     - **Prominence of confirmatory caveats vs. exploratory findings in Section 7.1:** The opening sentence of Section 7.1 (Conclusions, p. 17) leads with the statement that *"Visual distance predicted adult word extension across all eight trained encoders, extending the original finding beyond DINOv2,"* while relegating two critical qualifications from the frozen analysis plan to Footnote 1: (a) that the untrained `Random ResNet-50` control also yielded a statistically significant negative slope ($\beta = -1.10$, steeper than both `CVCL` at $-0.62$ and `MAE` at $-0.20$), meaning the positive-control slope cannot be attributed to learned features, and (b) that the frozen primary confirmatory test (trial-order moderation) was inconclusive ($\hat{\Delta} = 0.020$). Meanwhile, the remainder of the opening paragraph of Section 7.1 (including the boldfaced text) highlights the post-hoc exploratory 12-item comparison from Section 5.
   - **Substantive Impact:** Streamlining the procedural jargon, motivating the presentation-order hypothesis in the Introduction, and integrating the random-network control result and inconclusive primary test from Footnote 1 directly into the opening paragraph of Section 7.1 would substantially improve clarity for a cognitive science readership.

2. **Statistical and Theoretical Interpretation of the Untrained Control, Binary H1 Gating, and the Cross-System Synthesis (Abstract, p. 1; Section 1, pp. 2–3; Section 6, p. 16; Section 6.1, p. 17; Section 7.1, pp. 17–18)**
   - **Potential Concern:** There are three closely related tensions in how the Discussion and Conclusions synthesize the untrained network control, the H1/H2 human-alignment tests, and the artificial learner results:
     - **Equating binary significance with lack of separation on H1:** The Abstract (p. 1), Discussion (p. 16), Limitations (p. 17), and Conclusions (p. 18) treat the fact that both the trained encoders and the untrained network (Random ResNet-50) passed the binary H1 support threshold ($\rho > 0$, Mantel $p < .05$) as evidence that *"alignment with human similarity did not separate the trained encoders from the untrained network"* and *"does not show what the encoders learned."* However, inspecting the effect sizes and confidence intervals in Section 4.2 and Table 3 shows a large quantitative separation on the two primary similarity datasets: on THINGS ($3,400$ pairs) and ImageNet-HSJ ($1,390$ pairs), the non-autoencoder trained models achieve Spearman correlations of $\rho = .39$ to $.70$ ($15\%\text{--}49\%$ rank variance explained), whereas Random ResNet-50 achieves only $\rho = .11$ $[.08, .14]$ and $\rho = .11$ $[.05, .16]$ ($\approx 1\%$ variance explained, with non-overlapping confidence intervals), and raw pixels achieve $\rho = .15$ and $.20$. Because $N = 3,400$ and $N = 1,390$ provide high statistical power to detect trivial positive correlations, finding that $\rho = .11$ passes $p < .05$ (and reaches the lowest SESOI of $.10$) is an artifact of a zero-effect gating threshold rather than evidence that training fails to substantially increase human alignment.
     - **Unexplained mechanism of the random ResNet-50 vs. raw pixel contrast:** In the within-domain adult models, Random ResNet-50 yields a substantial negative distance slope ($\beta_{z(d)} = -1.10$, steeper than CVCL at $-0.62$ and MAE at $-0.20$), whereas raw pixels do not ($0.12$). While the text repeatedly notes that the adult slope *"cannot be attributed only to what the encoders learned,"* Section 6 does not discuss what architectural properties enable an untrained convolutional network to predict adult extension where raw pixels fail (e.g., hierarchical spatial pooling and multi-scale convolutional filter banks capturing translation-invariant foreground shape, color, and texture statistics in clean, center-cropped object photographs), nor why most trained encoders remain roughly three to four times steeper ($-3.16$ to $-4.30$ for DINOv2, SigLIP, OpenCLIP, and Supervised ViT) and far more accurate on CIFAR naming ($87.0\%\text{--}97.8\%$ vs. $41.0\%$ for Random ResNet-50).
     - **Tension between the "shared similarity space" conclusion, mechanical CIFAR gradients, and inconclusive H2:** Section 6 opens with the synthesis: *"Taken together, these results suggest that a shared, partly human-aligned similarity space describes part of the organization of generalization in both systems"* (p. 16). However, as acknowledged in Section 3.1 (p. 8), the artificial learner's accuracy drop across more distant CIFAR category pairs is *"close to mechanical for a learner that operates on the encoder's distances, so only the links tested in Section 4 are read against people"* (since placing a single prototype vector at the midpoint between two distant clusters in 3-way classification mechanically increases confusability with competing classes, and WordNet distance cannot add predictive power when the learner only takes visual embeddings as input). Because the direct test of whether human distances predict learner difficulty (H2) was inconclusive, the empirical bridge between the CIFAR disjunctive-word gradient and human hierarchical word extension is not established. Tempering the opening synthesis of Section 6 and distinguishing between architectural inductive biases and learned visual features would make the theoretical argument more cohesive.

3. **Internal Inconsistency Regarding Whether Optional Sensitivity Analyses Were Run (Section 6.1, p. 17; Appendix A, Table 8, p. 23; Appendix C, Tables 10–11, p. 25)**
   - **Potential Concern:** The final sentence of Section 6.1 (Limitations, p. 17)—immediately following a sentence discussing the Section 5 exploratory comparison on the same images—states unconditionally: *"Optional sensitivity analyses (MAE with CLS pooling, random-network seeds) were not run."* Furthermore, Table 8 (p. 23) states that *"In adults, these variants [MAE CLS pooling and random ResNet-50 seeds 1 and 2] exist only as sensitivity analyses of the primary test,"* whereas the note to Table 10 (p. 25) states *"Variants not run for these analyses: MAE with CLS pooling and random ResNet-50 seeds 1 and 2."*
   - **Substantive Impact:** While those variants were not run for the Section 2 adult mixed-effects models or the Section 3–4 CIFAR learner analyses (as noted in Appendix Tables 8, 10, and 11 on pp. 23 and 25), they *were* run and reported for the Section 5 exploratory matched-image comparison (see the bottom three rows of Table 4 on p. 14: `MAE (CLS)`, `Random ResNet-50 (s1)`, and `Random ResNet-50 (s2)`). Qualifying the sentence in Section 6.1 and clarifying in Table 8 and Table 10 that these optional sensitivity variants were not run *for the confirmatory adult mixed-effects and CIFAR learner analyses* (having been run only in the exploratory comparison of Table 4) would resolve the contradiction.

### [Adult Word Extension Reanalysis & Mixed-Effects Statistical Modeling] (Pages: 3-7, 21-25)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Conflation of Unweighted Centroid Distance ($d$) with "Visual Geometry" Across Teaching Conditions of Varying Dispersion (Abstract, p. 1; Section 1, pp. 1–2; Section 2.2, p. 4; Section 2.4, p. 6; Section 5, pp. 13–14; Section 6, p. 16; Section 7, pp. 17–18)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Throughout Section 2 and the overarching framing of the paper, visual similarity between a test item and the teaching examples on a trial is operationalized exclusively as $d$, the cosine distance between the test item's L2-normalized embedding and the L2-normalized centroid (mean) of the training examples shown on that trial (p. 4; paired with a static threshold in Section 5). In Section 2.4 (p. 6), the paper observes that for superordinate test items, adult selection rose from $1.1\%$ after three subordinate examples to $77.5\%$ after three superordinate examples, whereas mean centroid distances $d$ differed only modestly across the four teaching conditions ($.880, .892, .861,$ and $.845$ for one subordinate, three subordinate, three basic-level, and three superordinate examples, respectively). While Section 6 (p. 16) briefly notes a narrower qualification—that *"the distance-to-centroid measure we used does not, by itself, account for these differences between conditions"*—the headline takeaways in the Abstract (p. 1: *"Visual geometry predicts part of word extension across architectures, even without training, but does not capture the effect of example composition"*), Introduction (pp. 1–2), and Conclusions (Section 7.1, pp. 17–18) attribute the failure to capture the suspicious-coincidence and narrow-to-broad shifts to the visual spaces themselves rather than to a variance-blind readout. Relying solely on unweighted distance to the L2-normalized centroid conflates the limitations of a single-prototype summary statistic with the limits of the underlying representational geometry:
     - *Loss of dispersion information under L2 re-normalization:* On the unit hypersphere, averaging three tightly clustered embeddings (three subordinate examples, e.g., three Dalmatians) yields a pre-normalization mean vector $\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k u_i$ with a large Euclidean norm (near $1$) and small pairwise cosine distances, whereas averaging three widely dispersed embeddings (three superordinate examples, e.g., three different animals) yields a pre-normalization mean vector with a substantially smaller norm and large pairwise cosine distances. Re-normalizing the centroid onto the unit hypersphere discards all second-order geometric information in the encoder's space regarding the within-set dispersion (variance) of the teaching examples, as well as the sample size ($k=1$ vs. $k=3$).
     - *Comparison to established similarity and geometric generalization models:* Standard similarity-based and geometric accounts of categorization and the suspicious-coincidence effect in continuous spaces—such as exemplar models (e.g., Nosofsky, 1986, cited on p. 2, which sum nonlinearly decaying similarities over individual exemplars rather than computing distance to a prototype centroid), nearest-neighbor/minimum-distance rules ($\min_i d(x_{\text{test}}, x_i)$), and variance- or dispersion-scaled distance models (which normalize test distance by the spread of the observed examples)—explicitly use individual exemplar distances or within-set dispersion directly from the visual representation.
     - *Implications for Section 5 and Future Work (Section 7.2, p. 18):* Notably, when the agent in Section 5 uses the *support-dependent acceptance rule* in Table 4 (p. 14)—which presumably incorporates the spread of the support examples in the encoder's embedding space—several trained encoders exhibit a substantial narrow-to-broad shift (e.g., `Supervised ViT` increases within-domain acceptance from $0/12$ under `Same subtype` to $10/12$ under `Broad` while accepting $0/48$ other-domain images). Similarly, Section 7.2 proposes supplying explicit external information about *"relations among subtypes, basic types, and broader categories"* while holding the visual encoder fixed, overlooking that the visual embedding space already encodes the difference between low-dispersion (same-subtype) and high-dispersion (broad-domain) teaching sets if the learner attends to exemplar variance rather than only the mean vector.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Either include an exploratory sensitivity analysis in Section 2.4 testing whether exemplar-based similarity (e.g., minimum or exponentially summed cosine distance to individual training examples) or dispersion-scaled distance (scaling $d$ by the pairwise cosine dispersion among the three teaching examples or the pre-normalization centroid norm) accounts for part of the condition difference, or explicitly qualify the conclusions across the Abstract, Introduction, Section 2, Section 5, Section 6, and Section 7 to state that *unweighted distance to the normalized centroid*—rather than set-level visual geometry as a whole—does not capture example composition. Reporting the mean pairwise cosine distance (or pre-normalized centroid norm) among the three teaching examples in each condition would also clarify how much example dispersion is present in the encoder's geometry prior to centroid normalization.

2. **Unit-of-Analysis Conflict and Pseudoreplication Between the Inconclusive Experiment-Level Primary Test and the Observation-Level Post-Hoc Trial-Order Model (Section 2.3, pp. 4–5; Section 2.4, pp. 5–7; Section 6, p. 16; Appendix C, Table 10, p. 25)**
   - **Potential Concern:** There is an unreconciled methodological tension between the primary confirmatory test of presentation order and the post-hoc exploratory trial-order mixed model, driven by a shift in the unit of analysis, random-effects structure, and item set:
     - *Unit of analysis and omitted experiment-level clustering:* On pp. 4–5, the authors rightly note that trial presentation order (one-example trial first vs. last; six experiments each) varied only *between* the 12 experiments of Lewis and Frank (2018), and accordingly conduct inference for the primary test at the experiment level ($N = 12$ clusters), yielding an inconclusive slope difference for DINOv2-small ($\hat{\Delta} = 0.020$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.205, 0.274]$; Figure 1b; Table 10, p. 25). On pp. 6–7 (and in the Discussion on p. 16), however, the post-hoc trial-order model tests the same between-experiment factor (`order`) at the observation level ($N = 172,800$ item-level decisions across $520$ people) using a logistic mixed model with random effects grouped only by `person` and `image`, reporting highly significant likelihood-ratio tests for `z(d) × order` ($\chi^2(1) = 24.59, p < .001$, CI $[0.12, 0.28]$) and `z(d) × condition × order` ($\chi^2(3) = 29.12, p < .001$) and concluding that "moderation of the distance slope by order depended on the teaching condition" (p. 7). Testing a between-experiment factor ($N = 12$ clusters) and its cross-level interactions in an observation-level GLMM without random intercepts for `experiment` or by-experiment random slopes (`(1 + z(d) * condition | experiment)`) treats trial-level decisions and participants as independent replications of the experiment-level manipulation, inflating the effective degrees of freedom and Type I error rate.
     - *Item-set mismatch (8 within-domain items vs. all 24 items):* Whereas the positive-control framework in Figure 1a focuses on the 8 within-domain items ($57,600$ observations), the post-hoc trial-order model is fitted over all 24 items ($172,800$ observations), where the 16 out-of-domain items dominate the distance slope.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly explain in Section 2.4 (p. 7) and Section 6 (p. 16) why the observation-level post-hoc test produces $p < .001$ while the experiment-level primary test ($N = 12$) is wide and inconclusive—namely, the omission of experiment-level random effects (`(1 + z(d) * condition | experiment)`) in the observation-level likelihood-ratio test and the inclusion of all 24 items rather than the 8 within-domain items. Either re-estimate the post-hoc model with experiment-level random slopes on the 8 within-domain items or qualify the concluding sentences on p. 7 and p. 16 so readers do not interpret the unclustered observation-level $p < .001$ result as statistically overriding the experiment-level primary test.

3. **Domain-Contrast Confounding and Item-Set Mismatch in the 24-Item `distance × condition` Interaction Model vs. Figure 2a (Section 2.3, p. 4; Section 2.4, pp. 5–7; Appendix C, Table 10, p. 25)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 2.4 (pp. 5–6) and Table 10 (p. 25), the authors transparently demonstrate that in the 24-item H4 model (`selected ~ z(d) + level + condition + (1 + z(d) | person) + (1 | image)`), the 16 out-of-domain items were almost never selected and lie far from the training examples, so the 24-item distance coefficient is "dominated by the contrast between the taught domain and the other domains." In Table 10, this domain-contrast lever causes `MAE` to shift from the weakest within-domain slope ($\beta_{z(d)} = -0.20$ on 8 items) to the steepest slope of all representations ($\beta_{z(d)} = -7.56$ on 24 items), and raw `Pixels` to flip from $+0.12$ to $-2.43$. Two specific issues follow from this:
     - *Mismatch in the `z(d) × condition` interaction model:* Immediately after discussing the 8-item within-domain curves in Figure 2a (p. 6), the paper reports an exploratory `distance × condition` interaction model fitted over **all 24 items** ($\chi^2(3) = 2,134.2, p < .001$) to support the claim that "how strongly visual distance relates to extension depends on how the word was taught," finding that the slope was $-2.92$ after one example, changed little after three subordinate examples ($+0.10$), and became steeper after three basic-level ($-0.86$) and three superordinate examples ($-1.29$). In a 24-item model without a `level × condition` interaction, when within-domain superordinate selection jumps from $1.1\%$–$2.6\%$ to $77.5\%$ in the three-superordinate condition while the 16 out-of-domain items remain near $0\%$ ($0.34\%$), the logistic slope contrasting selected in-domain items against unselected out-of-domain items *must* become mechanically steeper simply to bridge the larger step-function gap between elevated in-domain selection and the out-of-domain floor. Visually, within the 8 target-domain items plotted in Figure 2a, the empirical curve for the three-superordinate condition (red/pink line) is actually *flatter* across distance than the subordinate and basic-level curves—the opposite of the steeper negative interaction coefficient ($-1.29$) estimated from the 24-item model.
     - *Unspecified coding of `level` across domains:* Section 2.3 (p. 4) does not specify how the fixed effect `level` is coded for the 16 out-of-domain items in the 24-item models versus the 8-item models (e.g., whether `level` has 3 levels coded across all domains by stimulus hierarchy, or 4 levels with an explicit out-of-domain category where near-zero selection induces quasi-complete separation).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Fit and report the exploratory `z(d) × condition` interaction model restricted to the **8 within-domain target items** ($N = 57,600$) alongside or in place of the 24-item model on p. 6 so that the condition-specific slopes directly match the within-domain generalization gradients plotted in Figure 2a without confounding from the 16 out-of-domain items. Additionally, state the exact factor levels of `level` in Section 2.3 for both the 24-item and 8-item models.

4. **Missing Mathematical Definitions of the Primary Permutation Test ($\hat{\Delta}$), Decision Regions, and "Geometry Calibration / Cutoffs" (Section 2.3, pp. 4–5; Section 2.4, p. 5; Section 4.2, p. 11; Section 7.1, pp. 17–18; Appendix C, Tables 10–11, p. 25)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Across Section 2.3 (pp. 4–5), Section 2.4 (p. 5), Section 4.2 (p. 11), Section 7.1 (pp. 17–18), and Appendix C (Table 10, p. 25), central multi-encoder claims rely on decision rules and calibration procedures that are not defined in the main text or Appendices A–C. Specifically, the primary test of presentation order is described only in a single sentence as "a two-stage permutation test of the slope difference $\hat{\Delta}$, classified into decision regions at three effect margins ($\delta = .05, .10, .20$) and summarized by a global rule across margins and geometry cutoffs," returning "G1 (inconclusive) at all 9 combinations of margin and geometry cutoff," and Section 4.2 (p. 11) states that *"Because the calibration across stimulus sets failed at every cutoff, the frozen global profile for H1 was 'holds for all, without evidence of a geometric difference'; these alignments do not show that the encoders differ in a human-relevant way."* Neither the main text nor Appendices A–C define:
     1. How Stage 1 and Stage 2 of the two-stage permutation test are mathematically constructed (e.g., whether separate GLMMs are fitted to each of the 12 experiments in Stage 1 on the 8 within-domain items or all 24 items, how $d$ is standardized at Stage 1, which order group is subtracted from which in $\hat{\Delta}$, and how the $95\%$ confidence intervals in Table 10 are generated across the $\binom{12}{6} = 924$ experiment assignments).
     2. What the three "geometry cutoffs" and "geometry calibration across stimulus sets" are (what metric they threshold, what the three cutoff values are that cross with $\delta \in \{.05, .10, .20\}$ to yield the 9 combinations, how calibration across stimulus sets is computed, and why its failure gates or invalidates encoder comparisons on H1).
     3. How the decision regions (`I`, `E` in Table 10; `S`, `I`, `N` in Table 11) and global rule (`G1`) are formally defined relative to the point estimates, $95\%$ confidence intervals, and margins $\pm\delta$.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Add a self-contained methodological specification in Section 2.3, Section 4.1, or Appendix A/C explicitly defining the Stage 1 and Stage 2 estimation equations for $\hat{\Delta}$, the item set used (8 vs. 24 items), the formal criteria mapping confidence intervals and $\pm\delta$ to decision regions (`I`, `E`, `S`, `N`), and the mathematical definition and cutoff values of the "geometry calibration" and global rule `G1` so that readers can evaluate the confirmatory framework without having to inspect external repository scripts.

5. **Cross-Encoder Slope Comparability Under Separate Within-Encoder Standardization and the MAE Random-Effects Fallback (Section 2.3, p. 4; Section 2.4, p. 5, Figure 1a; Appendix A, Table 8, p. 23; Appendix C, Table 10, p. 25)**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 2.3 (p. 4), distance $d$ is standardized ($z(d) = (d - \mu_d)/\sigma_d$) separately within each model and representation before fitting the logistic mixed models compared in Figure 1a (p. 5) and Table 10 (p. 25). Two statistical factors complicate direct comparisons of the magnitude and rank ordering of $\beta_{z(d)}$ across representations (such as noting on p. 5 that the randomly initialized ResNet-50 gave a steeper negative slope, $-1.10$, than CVCL, $-0.62$, and MAE, $-0.20$):
     - *Marginal vs. conditional log-odds scaling in the MAE fallback:* For `MAE` in the 8-item positive control, the model with by-person distance slopes failed convergence and non-singularity checks and was fitted with random intercepts only (`(1 | person) + (1 | image)`, omitting `(0 + z(d) | person)`; Figure 1a note, p. 5; Table 8, p. 23; Table 10, p. 25), whereas the other nine representations include by-person random slopes. In generalized linear mixed models with a logit link, fixed-effect log-odds coefficients are conditional on the random effects; omitting a random slope with non-zero variance systematically attenuates (shrinks toward zero) the fixed-effect log-odds coefficient toward the population-averaged marginal effect by a factor of approximately $\sqrt{1 + c^2 \sigma^2_{\text{slope}}}$ (where $c = \frac{16\sqrt{3}}{15\pi} \approx 0.588$). Thus, MAE's random-intercept-only slope ($-0.20$) is not on the same subject-conditional log-odds scale as the random-slope estimates of the other nine representations.
     - *Separate standardization across encoders:* While the text reports that $1\text{ SD}$ corresponds to $.219$ cosine units over 24 items and $.296$ over 8 items for DINOv2-small (p. 4), the raw cosine distance standard deviations ($\sigma_d$) for the other nine representations are not reported. Because different architectures and training objectives produce very different embedding cone anisotropies and dispersions, a $1\text{-SD}$ change in $d$ represents different geometric intervals across representations, and dividing by overall $\sigma_d$ while controlling for `level` and `condition` makes $\beta_{z(d)}$ sensitive to the ratio of between-level to within-level distance variance.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Report the raw cosine distance standard deviation ($\sigma_d$) for each representation in Table 10, and note in Section 2.4 and the Table 10 footnote that omitting the by-person random slope in the `MAE` fallback yields a less conditional log-odds estimate that is attenuated toward zero relative to models that include by-person random slopes.

6. **Omission of By-Image Random Slopes for Distance and Stimulus-Cell Clustering in the Superordinate-Only Model (Section 2.3, p. 4; Section 2.4, p. 6; Figure 2a, p. 7)**
   - **Potential Concern:** Across all adult GLMMs (`selected ~ z(d) + level + condition + (1 + z(d) | person) + (1 | image)`), random effects for stimuli are limited to a random intercept per test image `(1 | image)`, omitting both a by-image random slope for distance `(1 + z(d) | image)` and a stimulus-cell random intercept `(1 | image:condition)`.    A definitive inconsistency in the presentation and application of Smallest Effect Size of Interest (SESOI) decision regions (`S/I/N` for Support, Inconclusive, and No support across thresholds of `.10/.20/.30`, citing Lakens, 2017 and Schuirmann, 1987 on p. 8) appears across Appendix Tables 11 and 12 and directly affects statistical classifications in the main text:
   - **`Region D-all` in Table 11 (p. 25) and Main Text (p. 10):** Under standard equivalence and minimum-effect testing (Lakens, 2017; Schuirmann, 1987), classifying an effect into the "Support" (`S`) region relative to a specified SESOI threshold $\delta \in \{.10, .20, .30\}$ requires that the effect exceed the threshold $|\delta|$ in the predicted direction (specifically, that the confidence interval exclude $[-\delta, +\delta]$). 2. **Mechanistic Circularity and Theoretical Framing of the Artificial Learner Distance Effects (Section 3, pp. 7–10)**
   A potential conceptual and methodological concern in Section 3 is the tight mathematical coupling between the independent variable and the artificial learner's decision rule:
   - In Section 3.1 (p. 8), the main artificial learner is a cosine prototype (centroid) classifier operating on L2-normalized embeddings from a frozen vision encoder (Snell et al., 2017), and the auxiliary learners ($k$-nearest-neighbor and linear logistic regression) operate on the exact same frozen metric space. When a single disjunctive word is formed by pooling training examples from two categories into a single normalized centroid, increasing the cosine distance ($d_{\text{member}}$ or $d_{\text{perc}}$) between the two member categories in that *exact same* space geometrically pushes the midpoint prototype farther away from each constituent cluster, shrinking the classification margin relative to competing categories in a 3-way episode whenever individual classes are reasonably well separated. Conversely, for encoders where baseline single-category separation is poor—such as CVCL (baseline CIFAR-100 accuracy $.618$ vs. $.850$ for DINOv2-small on p. 12; $67.5\%$ native CIFAR-10 accuracy in Table 2) and MAE ($48.3\%$ native CIFAR-10 accuracy vs. $94.3\%$ for DINOv2-small in Table 2)—within-category variance dominates inter-centroid distance, yielding near-zero slopes in `D` ($r = .015$ and $r = -.004$).
   - Similarly, in the CIFAR-100 dissociation study (Section 3.1–3.2, pp. 8–9), the artificial learner receives *only* visual embeddings and has no access to WordNet synsets or CIFAR-100 superclass labels by construction. Consequently, finding that WordNet Wu–Palmer semantic distance ($p = 0.660$, partial $R^2 = 0.002$) and superclass membership ($\beta = 0.015, p = 0.528$) add no predictive power once the encoder's own embedding distance is controlled is an architectural property of a purely visual classifier rather than an empirical dissociation between perceptual and semantic word learning.
   - **Recommendation:** While Section 3.1 (p. 8) candidly notes that *"Distance effects in T and D are close to mechanical for a learner that operates on the encoder's distances"* and Section 3.2 frames the semantic test as a *"sufficiency result,"* Section 3, Figures 3–4, and Table 2 still frame `D` across the six additional trained encoders as a `"Confirmatory"` hypothesis test about word learnability. Reframing Section 3 (`T` and `D`) explicitly upfront as a diagnostic model-verification step—characterizing the cluster separability and prototype mechanics of each representation before linking those spaces to human judgments in Section 4—would better align the theoretical claims with the mechanics of the classifier.

2. **Discretization Resolution Limits ($1/12$ Step Size) and Operating-Point Trade-offs in Cross-System Comparisons (Section 5, pp. 13–15; Section 6.1, p. 17; Section 7.1, pp. 17–18)**
   - **Potential Concern Regarding Inferential Calibration:** Section 5 and Section 7.1 elevate the exploratory matched-image comparison to a headline conclusion (featured in the Abstract and the opening paragraph of Section 7.1). Although Section 5 ("Units of analysis," p. 13) and Section 6.1 (p. 17) acknowledge that each agent makes only 12 within-domain and 48 other-domain deterministic decisions per condition (*"so a single decision moves a within-domain rate by one twelfth"*), the quantitative interpretation in the text does not account for two consequences of this setup:
     - *Discrete step-size constraint in percentage-point comparisons:* Because $1/12 \approx 8.33$ percentage points, any deterministic agent operating on the 12 within-domain superordinate items can only produce rates and rate differences in discrete multiples of $8.33\%$ (e.g., $8/12 = 66.67\%$, $9/12 = 75.00\%$, $10/12 = 83.33\%$). An agent achieving $8/12$ ($66.67$ percentage points) is a single binary item decision away from $9/12$ ($75.00$ percentage points), which is within $1.43$ percentage points of the continuous adult difference ($76.43\%$)—the closest attainable discrete approximation on a 12-item grid. Concluding that *"no common acceptance threshold on any trained encoder reproduced the adults' gain from narrow to broad teaching (at most 66.67 vs. 76.43 percentage points)"* treats a one-item discrete step difference on a 12-item test set as evidence of a substantive representational gap.
     - *Sensitivity–specificity trade-offs across encoders and the single-item turtle case study:* When comparing `DINOv2-base` and `Supervised ViT` in Table 4 (p. 14) and Section 7.1 (p. 17), both accept $10/12$ within-domain candidates under broad teaching, but `DINOv2-base` accepts $20/48$ ($41.7\%$) other-domain images (and $12/48$ under same basic type, comparable to the untrained `Random ResNet-50` control at $38/48$ and `Pixels` at $20/48$), whereas `Supervised ViT` accepts $0/48$ other-domain images. This indicates that applying the uncalibrated support-dependent acceptance rule across encoders with different embedding anisotropy or cosine-distance distributions places them at very different false-positive operating points. Consequently, in the single-photograph case study of the turtle (`c3_sup2`, Table 6, p. 15), `DINOv2-base`'s acceptance of the turtle under both the animal and vehicle words reflects its high global out-of-domain false-alarm rate ($20/48$) under that rule rather than a specific representational link between turtles and vehicles.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Calibrate the discussion of the $66.67$ vs. $76.43$ percentage-point comparison in Sections 5 and 7.1 by explicitly noting that $8/12$ ($66.67\%$) is one item away from the nearest discrete step ($9/12 = 75.00\%$), and consider supplementing the thresholded counts with threshold-free continuous metrics (such as the area under the ROC curve separating within-domain superordinates from other-domain items across teaching conditions) so that differences in encoder score scaling are separated from category-boundary geometry.
